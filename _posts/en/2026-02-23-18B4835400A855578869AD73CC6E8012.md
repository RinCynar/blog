### The Wrath of the Observer

Beneath the cloying, sugary coating of "love," what I saw was absolutely not some poignant tragedy, but a highly self-consistent microcosm brutally formatted by a mediocre outside world.

When Satou and Shio surrendered to gravity on that blazing rooftop, what I heard wasn't some poetic elegy, but pure, unadulterated wailing from the scriptwriter's CPU burning out entirely in the face of extreme logic. In this essay, I want to completely rip the pants off this fake ending that flaunts "redemption" but is actually a cowardly "surrender."

---

### The Waste of the System Firewall

According to the most rigorous game-theory deduction, the ultimate "system firewall" buried at the base of the script was originally supposed to be the aunt.

She is the physical embodiment of "nihilistic love," possessing infinite bandwidth capable of swallowing all sins and external interference. The optimal solution dictated that her remaining in the inferno would serve a dual purpose: physically incinerating all "garbage data" like corpses, fingerprints, and social traces; and legally absorbing all the causality and crimes of the old world.

The moment the aunt embraced the fire, the external tracking link was completely severed. Satou and Shio had already achieved the erasure of their social identities through that "altar of fire." Since the system had undergone a deep clean and was ready for a reboot at any moment, enforcing a physical self-destruct sequence at the very end (having Satou fall to her death) was absolutely not a narrative inevitability. It was purely the author's mental self-castration.

### The Hypocrisy and Evasion of Redemption

The writers forcibly stuffed Satou into a mold of "sacrificing oneself to protect Shio," vainly attempting to sublimate it into some "maternal" or "saintly" redemption. To the pure logic of the yandere, this is a massive irony.

In the barren, narrow minds of the creators, Satou killed people and lied, so she "must" die. They crudely imported external secular morality, shoving it between the completely closed sovereign contract of two souls, insisting on lecturing the audience with a cheap message: *Evil love must be paid for with a life.*

More ironically, in this contract, Shio had long ceased to be an accessory; she was a true symbiote. Forcing Shio to "survive" actually stripped her of her right to participate in the final destruction as an equal half. She was left stranded on the ruins of "reality," forced to endure the chronic poisoning of what the secular world calls "growing up normally." This kind of lingering existence is the cruelest imprisonment—Shio was turned into a living tombstone, a safety warning sign erected for the eyes of the mediocre masses.

### The Absent Morning Flight

If one followed the logic of absolute sovereignty, the true ending should have been a clean, flawless victory.

Asahi's breakdown had already proven that all external interference was completely ineffective; his collapse was precisely because he realized he was utterly powerless to intervene. Satou and Shio should have calmly boarded that early morning flight, gazing down from tens of thousands of feet at the shrinking, noisy, rule-bound secular world below.

Even with hands drenched in blood, as long as they had established a "transfer of sovereignty" between them, their universe would have achieved perfect self-consistency. Love is absolutely not a moral accessory, but the ultimate contract of sovereignty.

---

### Gravity Strangled the Will

The reason the authors insisted on writing a tragedy is simply that they were terrified.

They were terrified that if they actually let Satou win, it would be tantamount to admitting to everyone: a transcendent, exclusive, solipsistic will can actually succeed in reality. To defend the childish fairy tale that "evil cannot triumph over good," they dragged out gravity, dragged out secular law, and dragged out that moldy set of moral concepts.

Satou didn't die from the fire, nor did she die from the fall; she died from the writers' impoverished imagination. That leap didn't just shatter bones; it shattered the logical height this work should have achieved. In a world of "I think, therefore I am," that morning flight never truly landed.

> *Watching Satou fall on screen was like watching a utopia collapse because it ran out of computing power. The writers thought they gave Shio a "future," ignorant of the fact that for someone who has witnessed absolute sovereignty, returning to a mediocre reality is the most bottomless hell of all.*
> *If I were the observer, I would personally sever the fuse of gravity. After all, compared to so-called moral correctness, extreme logical self-consistency is infinitely more fascinating.*
